Baseline: what does fraud cost if the merchant approves all transactions?

The data is now clean. Before we build a score, we must know the cost that the score must decrease. The simplest policy approves all transactions. It has no false declines and no reviews, so its total cost is the fraud loss.

The fraud loss of a transaction is its amount if it is fraud (as-01: the business pays 100% of the chargeback). The table gives the cost for each 30 days, so that months with a different number of days compare correctly.

So what: the fraud loss stays between $450,222 and $643,237 each 30 days, and the fraud rate stays between 2.48% and 4.04%. Fraud is a constant cost, not one bad month. Month 4 has the highest loss. On the test months 5–6, approve-all costs $470,983 each 30 days.

Business note. The data has 590,540 clean transactions, about 3,245 each day, with a fraud rate of 3.50%. Fraud has 3.87% of the dollars. Approve-all costs about $508,326 each 30 days: this is the ceiling of the fraud loss. Two facts limit what a score can promise: the customer key is a proxy, and the label of a customer can follow a later chargeback. Step 3 now splits the data by time, so that the test looks like production.

Branch 4: business value

The question: at the same review capacity, does the challenger decrease the total cost against the champion? This is the main business test. Both policies have the same three tiers and the same capacity of 120 alerts each day (as-03). Only the score and the value that ranks the transactions change:

  • Champion (as-06). The rules of a decision tree with 3 levels, fit on month 1. The code sets its decline and review thresholds on month 4, with the method of Step 5c.
  • Challenger. The GBM score after the recalibration, and the expected cost in dollars (Step 5d).

The guardrail is the decline rate of good transactions. A decline of a good customer has a cost in the model (as-02), but it also hurts the brand. Thus the rule adds a hard limit of 3% (as-08). Marketing must confirm this limit before the test.

On the test months, the challenger decreases the total cost by $126,820 each month (42%) against the champion, with the same review capacity. It stops 71.2% of the fraud dollars. The champion stops 38.4%.

The challenger declines more good transactions: 2.46% against 0.28% for the champion. This rate is inside the guardrail limit of 3%. The challenger passes branch 4.

The champion approves almost all transactions and accepts the fraud loss. With the cost of a false decline in as-02, this choice is expensive. The champion also has a capacity problem. Its rule segments change in size from day to day. Thus its alerts are more than the capacity on 48% of the test days, against 5% for the challenger.

Rollout plan

The result of Step 6 comes from past transactions. Fraud patterns, customers and costs change. A controlled test gives the proof before a full rollout. The plan has five parts.

1. Test design. Send a random 10% of transactions to the challenger. The other 90% stay with the champion. A random allocation makes the two arms equal in all other ways, so the difference comes from the policy.

Each arm gets the same share of the review capacity as of the transactions. Thus the challenger arm gets 10% of the alerts each day, and the test keeps the condition “same review capacity”.

The test must run long enough to find the difference in cost. The code estimates the number of days from the cost of each transaction on months 5–6. It uses a significance level of 5% (two-sided) and a power of 80%.

The challenger arm gets about 286 transactions and 12 alerts each day. The challenger saves $1.48 for each transaction. To find this difference, the test needs about 16 days of transactions. Then it needs time for the chargebacks. The data has no chargeback dates, so Fraud Operations must give this delay.

2. Main KPI. The total cost for each 1,000 transactions in each arm, after the chargeback delay. The total cost includes the fraud loss, the false declines and the review friction (Step 5b). The cost for each 1,000 transactions makes the two arms of different size comparable.

3. Guardrails. Check these values each week:

  • The decline rate of good transactions of the challenger arm: 3% or less (as-08). The label arrives late, so the decline rate of all transactions is the early signal.
  • The alerts each day against the capacity of the arm.
  • The PSI of the score against months 1–3, and the PR-AUC of each month with labels (Part 2).

4. Kill criteria. Stop the test in any of these cases:

  • The decline rate of all transactions in the challenger arm is above 3% for two weeks in a row.
  • The fraud loss for each 1,000 transactions of the challenger arm is above the champion arm for one full month with labels.
  • The PSI of the score is more than 0.25.

5. Top risks.

Risk Effect Control
The cost of a false decline (as-02) is higher than the assumption. The challenger declines too many good customers. The decrease of the total cost is too high. Finance and Marketing confirm as-02 and as-08 before the test. The guardrail and the kill criteria stop the test.
The fraud patterns change after the training months. The score loses power, but the PSI stays low (Step 6c). Retrain each month (Part 2). Check the PR-AUC of each month with labels.
Fraud operations cannot read why the policy declines (branch 5). A partner complaint has no short answer. Fraud operations cannot check the declines. Give the surrogate rules to fraud operations as patterns, not as reasons. If a partner asks for reasons, use the alternative of Step 4b.
The champion is an assumption (as-06). The real champion can be stronger or weaker. The decrease of the total cost changes. The test measures the real champion, because the 90% arm uses it.
The labels arrive late (as-07). The test result and each retrain use labels that are not complete. Read the KPI only after the chargeback delay. Train on all months except the last one when the labels are late.

Decision at the end. If the challenger arm has a lower total cost for each 1,000 transactions and stays inside the guardrail, roll out the challenger. Increase the share step by step and keep the champion arm as a control.

Business note. At the current capacity, the challenger decreases the total cost by $126,820 each month. In each cost case that we tested, the decrease is at least $53,544. The best team size changes the cost by only $1,534 each month.

The recommendation is a test on 10% of transactions for about 16 days, with the guardrail of 3% (as-08). The test uses one score with no retrain. Part 2 examines how fast this score loses power, and when to retrain it.

The champion is an assumption (as-06). We built it because the data has no real rules. The gain of the challenger depends on the champion: $64,274 to $126,820 each month. The ladder of four champions in Step 6d. Both policies use the same review capacity and cost method. The test of Step 7c measures the gain against the real rules.

The PSI does not find a change of the fraud patterns. A score can lose value with a stable PSI. Check the PR-AUC of each month with labels. Retrain each month (dec-07).
The label includes later transactions of a customer after a chargeback (as-07). The history features can look stronger than in production. History from earlier transactions only (dec-02).
