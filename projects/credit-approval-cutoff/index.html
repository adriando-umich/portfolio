Q: Can a new approval cutoff increase approvals at the same bad rate?

Yes. A scorecard cutoff approves more applicants at the same bad rate and adds $7.1M of realized NPV on the 2015 loans.

Why? Three reasons, strongest first

  1. It approves more applicants with no more defaults

    Approval rate: 85.7% vs. 84.5% today. Bad rate (default rate of approved loans): 12.5%, held equal.

  2. The gain holds under every cost assumption tested

    Realized NPV against the current policy: +$7.1M. Lowest result in the stress test: +$5.1M.

  3. A small test limits the risk of the change

    Start on 10% of applications. Stop if 12-month defaults rise 1 point.

The 2015 loans lost money under the current policy; the new cutoff recovers $7.1M

The framework behind the answer

The problem

Starting point

A consumer lender offers unsecured 36-month loans. A risk grade puts each applicant in a sub-grade from A1 to G5. The lender approves the best sub-grades and declines the others.

Result now

The business wants more approvals, but Risk does not accept a higher bad rate. The loans from 2015 lost $32.9M.

Result wanted

More approvals at the same bad rate of 12.5%, and more realized NPV from the same applicants.

The story in four lines

SituationWhat the reader knowsA consumer lender approves applicants by credit grade. Loans from 2013–2014: realized NPV +$71.7M.
ComplicationWhat changed, and what it costIn 2015, interest rates fell and default rates rose in every grade. Loans from 2015: realized NPV –$32.9M.
QuestionThe decisionQ: How can the lender approve more customers with no increase in losses?
AnswerThe recommendation and its effectReplace the credit grade with a risk scorecard. Approval rate 84.5% → 85.7% at a bad rate of 12.5%. Realized NPV +$7.1M. Next step: a test on 10% of applications.

The questions that the analysis must answer

Is the new cutoff good enough to test on real applications?

  1. Does the scorecard approve more applicants at the same bad rate?

    Yes. Approval rate 85.7% vs. 84.5%, bad rate 12.5% for both.

  2. Do the swapped applicants make money?

    Yes. The applicants that the scorecard approves in place of others add $8.3M of realized NPV.

  3. Does the gain hold when the cost assumptions change?

    Yes. At least +$5.1M under every cost assumption tested.

  4. Is a more complex model worth it?

    No. A gradient boosting model ranks risk only 1.5 KS points better, below the 2-point rule set before the test. The scorecard stays, and it gives a reason for each decline.

  5. Does the cutoff alone fix the loss on the 2015 loans?

    No. The cutoff recovers $7.1M of a $32.9M loss. A pricing test for grades A to C must go with it.

Both policies run on the same 2015 applicants, and every number is an actual outcome

  1. Define the current policy on booked loans

    The current policy approves the best sub-grades, up to about 85% of the 2015 applicants.

  2. Use the declined group as the check group

    The lender booked loans in the lowest sub-grades too. Each of these loans has a real outcome: default or no default, and the cash it returned.

  3. Compare the two policies on actual outcomes

    The scorecard approves the same number of applicants in a different order. The comparison uses the actual default rate and the realized NPV of the applicants that change sides.

  • The scorecard learns from the loans of 2013–2014 and is tested on the loans of 2015, which it never saw.
  • Grade, sub-grade and interest rate are not inputs to the scorecard, because they are results of the lender's own policy.
  • The current policy is my assumption. The public data shows only booked loans, not the lender's approval rule. The result shows a method and the likely size of the gain. The test on 10% of applications measures the gain against the real policy.
